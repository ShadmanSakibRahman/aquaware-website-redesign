Article24 August 2025·8 min read

Discharge permits are often outdated or incomplete

“Outdated permits make inspection harder – reviewing them brings control and clarity.”

AquaWare · 24 August 2025 LinkedIn

Introduction

Discharge permits provide the legal basis for regulating emissions of substances to water. They determine which substances may be discharged, in what quantities and under what conditions. This makes them an indispensable instrument for public authorities to protect water quality and give companies clarity.

Yet many permits do not always reflect current reality. Some are outdated and take insufficient account of new substances or processes; others are incomplete and lack provisions needed for effective inspection. The result: companies operate in accordance with the rules, yet harmful substances still enter the water system via legal routes.

Permits that lag behind

Many discharge permits date from a time when knowledge of certain substances, or attention to diffuse discharges, was more limited than it is now.

  • New substances out of view – Substances such as PFAS or other substances of very high concern (ZZS) are often not included in older permits, because they were not yet on the radar at the time.
  • General conditions – Permits sometimes contain generic standards that are not specifically tailored to the type of company or the substances released there.
  • Slow to adapt – Permits are not always reviewed in good time when processes or substances change. As a result, a permit may no longer reflect the current situation.

This creates a gap between reality on paper and ecological reality.

Incompleteness: not everything is covered

  • Limited substance lists – Many permits still focus on traditional substances such as nutrients or heavy metals, while countless other compounds are barely covered.
  • Too little focus on indirect routes – Permits often focus mainly on direct discharges. Indirect discharges via sewers and wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) are taken into account less explicitly, even though they make up a large part of the load.
  • Missing basic data – Data on quantities or concentrations is not always fully available, which makes inspection more difficult.

This means that inspectors do not always have the full set of tools they need to steer effectively.

Consequences for water quality and policy

  • Discharges within the rules – Companies formally discharge in line with their permits, yet harmful substances still end up in the environment.
  • Gaps in inspection – Without a complete picture, inspectors lack the leads they need to spot risks in time.
  • Missed opportunities for the Water Framework Directive objectives – When permits are not up to date, it becomes harder to achieve the objectives of the Water Framework Directive (WFD) for chemical and ecological water quality by 2027.
  • Trust and transparency – Clear, up-to-date permits strengthen the confidence of companies and citizens that discharges take place responsibly.

Conclusion

Discharge permits are a powerful instrument, but only when they are up to date and complete. In practice, many permits turn out to be outdated or incomplete, which makes inspection and water protection more complex than necessary. The urgency is clear: by reviewing permits and aligning them more closely with today’s knowledge and substances, authorities gain more control over discharges and companies can work within the rules with greater clarity. This creates a solid foundation for future-proof water management and increases the likelihood that the WFD objectives will be achieved on time.
