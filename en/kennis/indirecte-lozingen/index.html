Indirect discharges: from blind spot to clear picture

“Indirect industrial discharges are often less visible, but through collaboration and smart data linking, public authorities can gain more control and work more effectively towards the WFD objectives.”

AquaWare · 20 August 2025 LinkedIn

Introduction

When people think of industrial discharges, they often picture the classic image of a factory pipe discharging directly into a river. In reality, that is only part of the story. A significant share of industrial emissions takes place via indirect routes: wastewater that flows through municipal sewers or industrial systems to wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs), and from there still ends up in surface water.

These indirect discharges are often less visible. They involve many different companies and come together in complex networks of pipes and installations. That makes it harder to obtain a complete overview. The result is a route that has a major influence on water quality, but that requires extra attention to map out properly.

Why indirect discharges are difficult to see

There are several reasons why indirect industrial discharges are less visible:

  • Historical focus on direct dischargers — For decades, the emphasis in permitting and monitoring was on large companies that discharge directly. Indirect routes were more fragmented and therefore less systematically mapped.
  • Dispersed information — Data is spread across different parties: municipalities, water authorities, provinces and environmental agencies, each of which holds valuable pieces of the puzzle. Bringing that information together is complex, but it is precisely what creates opportunities for new insights.
  • Permits in need of updating — Some indirect discharges still fall under older or generic permits in which new substances such as PFAS or other substances of very high concern (SVHCs) are addressed less explicitly.
  • Complex flows — Wastewater from dozens of smaller companies often comes together in a single pipe. That makes it difficult to trace exactly where a particular substance comes from.

These factors make it challenging to bring indirect discharges fully into view. But this is also exactly where there is great potential to gain more control through smart analysis and cooperation.

The role of WWTPs and industrial estates

WWTPs play a central role in this issue. They are designed to reduce nutrients and organic load, but not to fully break down persistent substances or heavy metals. For SVHCs, they therefore often act as a transit point: the substances pass through the treatment process and end up in the effluent.

Industrial estates in particular require extra attention here. Hundreds of small companies discharge collectively through a single industrial sewer system. Individually the emissions appear limited, but together they represent a considerable load. A WWTP mainly sees the sum total, without it being immediately clear which companies contribute the most.

The governance puzzle

In addition to the technical challenges, there is also the complexity of governance. Various parties each have an important role to play. Each party holds crucial information and expertise. The challenge lies in bringing that knowledge together and creating an integrated picture. It is precisely through cooperation and data sharing that blind spots can be reduced and supervision can be organised even more effectively.

Consequences and urgency

  • Environmental risks — SVHCs accumulate in sediment, soil and food chains. Even low emissions can have major effects over time.
  • Health impact — Many SVHCs are carcinogenic, endocrine-disrupting or neurotoxic. Invisible emissions increase the likelihood that risks are recognised only at a late stage.
  • Strengthening cooperation and trust — Achieving the Water Framework Directive (WFD) objectives by 2027 is a shared task. By linking information intelligently and intensifying cooperation, public authorities can further strengthen their role as reliable coordinators of water quality. This increases not only the effectiveness of policy and supervision, but also the confidence of citizens and businesses in future-proof water management.

Conclusion

Indirect industrial discharges are one of the greatest challenges in water management today. They are fragmented, diffuse and embedded in complex technical and governance structures, which makes them less visible. That is precisely why attention to this route is so important. By connecting data, sharing information and working together, authorities can gain more control over these discharges. This not only provides a clearer view of SVHCs and other harmful substances, but also a stronger foundation for future-proof water management. The urgency is clear: by working together on insight and transparency, authorities can act more effectively and work with confidence towards achieving the WFD objectives.
